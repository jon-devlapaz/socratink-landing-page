# Mechanism evidence register

Use this register to avoid treating popular behavioral claims as universal laws. Grades apply only to the narrow claims stated here. Direct evidence from the user’s own audience can override general priors when the research design is credible.

## 1. Evidence table

| Mechanism | Default grade | Narrow claim supported | Boundary and safe test | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Reference dependence and anchoring | supported | Relevant numeric context can shift subsequent estimates and evaluations. | Attention, knowledge, and anchor relevance matter; a bare arbitrary number may do nothing. Compare relevant high, low, and reference-free contexts; reject fake prior prices. | [Mussweiler & Strack, 1996](https://doi.org/10.1037/0096-3445.125.4.387); [replication-oriented boundary evidence](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.602372) |
| Asymmetric choice / decoy | promising | A clearly dominated option can shift choice toward the dominating target. | Effects depend on understandable dominance, near-indifference, and weak prior preference. Use a no-decoy control and measure actual fit and selection; reject obstructive or misleading choice sets. | [Meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1086/209449); [boundary experiment](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joep.2019.05.007) |
| Price–quality inference | supported | Higher price can increase perceived quality in some consumer-product contexts. | This supports perceived quality, not objective quality or durable satisfaction. Hold delivery constant and test price with and without verifiable quality evidence. | [Rao & Monroe, 1989](https://doi.org/10.1177/002224378902600309) |
| Scarcity | promising | Credible scarcity can increase desirability or perceived value. | Effects are heterogeneous and source credibility matters. Test only real capacity, inventory, cohort, or time constraints; include complaints, cancellation, trust, and repeat behavior. | [Quantitative review](https://doi.org/10.1002/mar.4220080105) |
| Social proof / descriptive norms | supported | Truthful social norms can influence purchase, use, consumption, or disposal behavior. | Descriptive and injunctive norms differ; audience and behavior fit matter. Test a truthful local norm against neutral information and avoid broadcasting undesirable low participation. | [Consumer-behavior meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1177/00222429211029199) |
| Self-assembly / IKEA effect | supported | Completed self-assembly increases valuation on average in the studied literature. | A 55-study meta-analysis reports a moderate effect (`d = 0.57`); do not generalize to failed assembly, burdensome setup, or all co-creation. Measure abandonment, effort, satisfaction, and valuation together. | [Pelled, Demetriades & Walter, 2025](https://doi.org/10.1002/mar.70064) |
| Processing fluency and naming | promising | Easier perceptual or conceptual processing can improve evaluation in some contexts. | Disfluency can help where stimulation or deliberation is valued. Hold comprehension constant and test pronunciation or presentation with the intended audience. | [Lee & Labroo, 2004](https://doi.org/10.1509/jmkr.41.2.151.28665); [boundary review](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.copsyc.2016.01.008) |
| Sensory, material, and weight cues | promising | Material and visual cues can change inferred physical properties. | General quality and value effects are category-specific. Use identical-function prototypes and measure actual usability, accessibility, waste, and shipping cost as well as perception. | [Material-perception review](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-vision-102016-061429); [systematic visual-cue review](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.foodqual.2023.104996) |
| Choice overload and defaults | supported | Complexity, task difficulty, preference uncertainty, and defaults can alter choice. | Effects are heterogeneous and can be null or negative. Test set size and complexity separately; defaults must be disclosed, reversible, and fit-based. | [Choice-overload meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jcps.2014.08.002); [default meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1017/bpp.2018.43) |
| Peak–end experience memory | supported | Peaks and endings disproportionately affect retrospective evaluations across studied experiences. | This is not proof of retention, loyalty, or permission to neglect the rest of an experience. Preserve total service quality, measure delayed recall and behavior separately, and account for the published corrigendum. | [Alaybek et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.obhdp.2022.104149); [corrigendum](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0749597823000547) |

## 2. Application rules

1. Start with the diagnosed gap, then select a mechanism.
2. Narrow the claim to the mechanism’s demonstrated outcome. Perceived quality is not objective quality; retrospective evaluation is not retention.
3. Check the boundary condition against the subject, audience, and decision context.
4. Prefer direct customer and operational evidence over generic priors when credible.
5. Convert a mechanism into a falsifiable test; do not write it as a guaranteed tactic.
6. Apply the truth, autonomy, accessibility, and harm gates before ranking.
