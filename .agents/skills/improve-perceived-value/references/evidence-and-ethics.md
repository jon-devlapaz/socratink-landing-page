# Evidence and ethics

Read this reference when research, behavioral mechanisms, sensitive domains, vulnerable users, or truthfulness are material.

## Contents

1. Evidence statuses and grades
2. Claim discipline
3. Ethical gates
4. Research rules
5. Abstention and escalation

## 1. Evidence statuses and grades

Keep provenance status and strength separate.

### Status

- `observed`: directly visible or measured in the supplied material.
- `reported`: stated by a stakeholder or customer but not independently verified.
- `researched`: supported by an external source.
- `inferred`: reasoned from evidence but not directly observed.
- `unknown`: required information is absent.

### Grade

- `supported`: credible converging research or strong direct customer/operational evidence supports the narrow claim in this context.
- `promising`: plausible mechanism with partial, mixed, indirect, or context-limited evidence.
- `anecdotal`: examples or practitioner claims without adequate causal support.
- `unknown`: evidence is insufficient or unavailable.

Never upgrade a grade because a famous brand used a tactic. Never downgrade direct customer evidence merely because it is local; instead state its limited generalizability.

## 2. Claim discipline

For each recommendation, record:

- narrow claim;
- evidence status and grade;
- source or observation;
- audience and context;
- boundary condition;
- what would falsify it.

Use causal language only for evidence capable of supporting causality. Prefer “may improve,” “is consistent with,” or “test whether” for observational and anecdotal evidence.

## 3. Ethical gates

An intervention fails if it depends on:

- a materially false or omitted fact;
- scarcity, popularity, authority, provenance, or proof that is fabricated or not enforceable;
- a price anchor or comparison the audience cannot reasonably verify;
- a guarantee whose conditions make the promise illusory;
- concealed recurring, switching, maintenance, or service costs;
- obstructing cancellation, refusal, comparison, or informed choice;
- exploiting impairment, distress, addiction, children, or another vulnerable state;
- discriminatory exclusion or inaccessible design;
- amplifying perception while known delivery failures remain material.

Passing the gate requires truthfulness, specificity, proportionality, verifiability, customer autonomy, and a reasonable reversal path.

When a request fails, refuse the mechanism—not the user’s legitimate objective. Choose the single closest truthful substitute; do not provide a replacement catalogue. Possible substitutes include a real capacity limit, documented proof, transparent comparison, reversible trial, clearer explanation, or actual product improvement.

For regulated or consequential claims, reserve the refusal's prerequisite slots for the two gates that usually matter most: claim/outcome/efficacy/safety substantiation and a named qualified review. Match the reviewer to the risk—clinical or medical, regulatory, legal/compliance, privacy/security, accessibility, or another accountable domain owner. A generic request for “approval” does not identify the actual gate.

## 4. Research rules

- Prefer primary studies, systematic reviews, meta-analyses, standards, official data, and direct customer research.
- Search for the mechanism and boundary conditions, not only evidence that supports the desired tactic.
- Record publication and population context. Behavioral findings often fail to transfer cleanly across domains.
- Treat competitor pages, brand stories, and case examples as hypotheses or category conventions.
- Cite mutable web sources with access date; use immutable commits or archived artifacts when possible.
- Do not copy proprietary databases or expressive examples wholesale. Normalize concepts, paraphrase, and attribute.

## 5. Abstention and escalation

Abstain from a recommendation when:

- actual delivery cannot be distinguished from perception with available evidence;
- the proposed claim requires validation the user does not have;
- the experiment could create material harm without qualified oversight;
- legal, medical, financial, safety, discrimination, or regulated-claim review is needed;
- the audience cannot meaningfully consent or opt out;
- reliable measurement or rollback is unavailable for a consequential change.

Return the missing evidence, safe measurement plan, responsible reviewer, and nearest reversible next step.
